Android at G-STAR

Decision question

Which Android AI use cases should a booth show, and why would a gamer choose it over the biggest game brands?

Result +17pp purchase intent among non-Android users (OKR target was +13pp), and 2× visitors year over year.

How the booth was built

ListenPython crawler on 4,646 prior-year community posts, classified across five keyword categories. Plus a 75-person survey.“Queue” topped at 358 mentions. 53% come for real rewards; they plan routes by queue speed.
Test with GIF simulations10 non-Android gamers shown AI features as GIFs, not live builds. One week of research.70%+ wanted Circle to Search for content around games, not during gameplay.
Score and cutPriority = 0.6 × likelihood of use + 0.4 × brand consideration. Cases below the baseline on either metric were dropped.5 use cases kept, 3 cut.
Design around the dataTop cases front-loaded so every visitor sees them. Speed game turns the #1 use case into play. Google Play Points as headline reward. Layouts simulated for queue speed.+17pp purchase intent (target: +13pp), 2× visitors YoY.
Audience insight

I interviewed 10 heavy mobile gamers (non-Android users, 18 to 35) in person and showed Android AI features as GIF simulations. The team expected demand for Circle to Search during gameplay. That was wrong. Over 70% wanted it to find items in gaming content, and over 50% wanted it to translate Japanese webtoons. Gamers wanted help across everything they watch and read around games.

To learn why people visit at all, I wrote a Python crawler to analyze 4,646 posts from the prior year’s G-STAR board on the largest gamer community. I designed a five-category keyword taxonomy—rewards, visit motivation, booth experience, brand mentions and negative reactions—and ran automated classification across every post. “queue” dominated at 358 mentions: visitors planned their routes by queue speed and reward value. Only 0.7% of posts were negative. I also ran a 75-person survey in the same community. That community skews toward heavy fans, so I read the numbers as direction, not as the share of all visitors. 53% came mainly for real rewards.

Fastest test

I had one week for research, so I picked the quickest method for each question. GIF simulations stood in for real builds, and an automated crawler I wrote stood in for on-site observation—I analyzed the prior year’s community data instead of waiting for live posts. A use case had to clear a baseline on both purchase-consideration uplift and relevance to make the final list.

What changed

I dropped the in-game demo and built the booth around the top-ranked use cases, shown early so every visitor met them. Google Play Points became the headline reward, because gamers value them like cash. I coordinated the agency to design the booth structure and contents, shifting to goal-oriented communication when I saw they needed clearer briefs. With the design team I simulated layouts to speed up turnover. The OKR target was +13pp consideration uplift—the booth delivered +17pp.

Delight moment

A three-player Circle to Search speed game. Players solve real gamer problems with the feature, then win rewards that scale with their rank. Gamers got competition and a payoff, and learned Android AI along the way.

Online extension

Not everyone could get to the venue, so I also built a mini-site for Google Play Points Platinum and Diamond members who could not visit G-STAR (Nov 14 to 17). Members hunted for hidden treasure-map pieces in a video, entered them to complete a map, and could win Play Points, gaming gear, a Play Pass subscription or an on-site gift. The mini-site reached more users than the offline booth, but it lifted brand consideration less. The trade-off was reach online against depth in person.

What I would do next

The social listening came from one community, the sample was small, and watching a GIF is not the same as using the phone. I would pilot the ranking on real devices to cross-check it.

Android × LE SSERAFIM

Decision question

Android had a K-pop fandom's attention. How does a tech brand earn their love?

Result 4.58M+ engagements at 98% positive sentiment. Coupang’s first-ever #1 Galaxy pre-sales ranking. Launched in 4 markets.

How the product took shape

ListenSocial listening + pop-up, concert, and collab café case studies.78% of organic buzz came from the artists’ own mentions. Fans reach for artist logo + sleek look.
Model three optionsFive fully custom bots vs. one base bot + five artist accessories vs. one generic bot. Costed each for production, labor, and engagement.Middle option: −80% cost vs. full custom, higher projected engagement than simplest.
Flip the hierarchyFirst packaging led with Android branding. Fan feedback was lukewarm. Reversed: LE SSERAFIM comes first, Android shows through a window at the mascot.Two “behind the design” artist interviews drew 32K views with no paid boosting.
Scale market by marketPitched each market separately. Joining was voluntary, so each market had to be persuaded by the case, not by mandate.4 markets, each with its own partner and mechanic.
Audience insight

Social listening showed that the artists’ own mentions drove 78% of organic buzz around Android. Case studies at pop-ups, a concert and a collaboration café showed fans reaching for items that clearly carried the artist’s logo and a sleek look. This insight drove the decision to flip the branding hierarchy and design a product fans would carry proudly.

Fastest test

Prototypes from v0 to v2, each reviewed in turn by users and experts, the brand team and the artist team. More than 20 plush samples were tested for quality and brand fit, and artist-design options were simulated to balance production cost, labor and customization.

What changed

Fan feedback on the first packaging, which led with Android branding, was lukewarm. I flipped the hierarchy so LE SSERAFIM comes first and Android shows through a window at the mascot. Five fully custom bots were too costly, so I modeled three options and chose one base bot with five artist-designed accessories, which cut production cost by about 80% against full customization and still beat the simplest option on projected engagement.

Fans’ favorite design elements went into the mascot and the packaging. During a countryside retreat in Hwaseong, the artists used Circle to Search to explore what they saw—wildflowers, butterflies, pumpkins, rural homes—and those became the sticker designs. The technology was woven into the creative story fans already wanted to tell.

Delight moment

A slow-opening, multi-layered unboxing that builds anticipation, and a mascot fans customize with accessories the artists designed. I led two “behind the design” artist interviews that drew 32K views with no paid boosting, showing how the artists used Circle to Search during their retreat to find design inspiration.

Fans carried it further on their own. On the fan board of the artists’ community app, members posted photos of the bot the day it appeared, one fan made a UFO charm from the keyring, and a fan’s X post about the collab drew 340 likes and 76 reposts.

Launch and markets

I led the project end to end—from overall strategy and merch development through agency coordination (HEAZ and HAPE) to global scaling—as main project manager. We launched in Korea first, where our partner Coupang used the kit as a Galaxy S25 Edge pre-order incentive and achieved its first-ever #1 ranking in Galaxy pre-sales. We then scaled to Japan, Brazil and Indonesia, each with its own partner and timing, as the map above shows.

I also led two “behind the design” artist interviews (32K views, no paid boosting) and guided authentic posts from the artists’ own accounts to shape the social campaign. The green Android bot appeared in the artists’ “Come Over” music video in March 2025, and the total campaign drove 4.58M+ engagements at 98% positive sentiment.

Joining was voluntary in every market, so I had to make the case to global Android marketing, the head of global Platforms & Ecosystems marketing and the brand team, and each market that took part had been persuaded. The plush later became the first main visual in the teaser for Android’s second LE SSERAFIM collaboration on Android’s global Instagram (watch the reel), and the artists featured the keyring in a joint post with Android that passed 100K likes.

SARABOME

Decision question

Only 9% of registered students shared a review. Why, and what would change it?

Result 3.6× core feature engagement and 200+ initial users, built solo in 4 months.

I built SARABOME, a review platform where Korea University students share the real condition of off-campus rooms—pests, noise, leaks, natural lighting, delivery access—in a structured Q&A format tied to each address. I started it after two rental contracts where the problems only showed up once I had moved in.

How the problem was traced

Ship and observeBuilt the MVP around 3 criteria renters care about: pests, noise, leaks. Launched to Korea University students.200+ registered, but only 9% shared a review.
Find the drop-offFirebase funnel analysis showed where users stopped.80% never clicked “Share Review.” Half who clicked quit before submitting.
Diagnose whyInterviews with non-sharers + a call with a housing-review CEO.Two blockers: “I came only to read” + “the landlord might see it.”
Fix the systemStudent email verification locks landlords out. Reciprocal unlock: full reviews visible only after you share yours.3.6× core feature engagement.
Audience insight

Interviews and house-hunting observation showed that first-time renters did not know what to check, and that experienced renters kept coming back to pests, noise and leaks. Once the MVP was live, funnel data showed 80% of visitors never clicked Share Review, and half of those who did quit before submitting.

Interviews with non-sharers found two blockers: they had come only to read, and some feared the landlord would see their review. A call with the CEO of a general housing review site confirmed that landlords were pressing platforms to delete reviews.

Fastest test

I shipped a working MVP first, built around the three criteria students cared about. Real usage then showed where the problem was, and I traced it with Google Analytics, interviews and the expert call.

What changed

Two changes to the system itself. Sign-up now needs a verified student email address, which keeps landlords out. Full reviews unlock only after you share your own, so reading and contributing are no longer separate.

Delight moment

Relief. A student sees what the last tenant lived through before signing, and feels safe enough to leave the same for the next one.

What I would do next

I shipped both changes together to move fast, so I could not tell which one did the work. I would test them separately, and look for a reason to come back outside moving season.

I answer them with audience insight, including large-scale voice-of-customer data, and then I launch fast and learn. That is how I worked through ambiguity at a startup, on a three-person team at Google Android, and on SARABOME, a housing platform I built from zero.

I have lived in six cities, and I learned early that people are often shut out by how things are built. A school with ramps and elevators made me an equal peer. A school without them left me on the outside. I notice who an experience leaves out, and what makes someone feel they belong.
